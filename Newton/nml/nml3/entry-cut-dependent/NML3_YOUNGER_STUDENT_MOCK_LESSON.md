# NML3 Mock Lesson — Adding Forces and Building a Force Account

**Status:** mock younger-student lesson draft  
**Project:** VD Newton / NML3  
**Audience:** upper primary / early secondary, adaptable upward  
**Core topic:** adding force vectors to obtain net force  
**Distinctive NML3 ending:** the vectors in a force sum are not arbitrary pieces of the resultant; they are meant to represent the physical interactions acting on the target object.

---

## 0. One-Sentence Thesis

**Several forces can act on one object at the same time. We add their force arrows to obtain one net-force arrow—but the forces we add must be the forces that actually belong to the object's interaction account, not just any arrows that happen to add to the right answer.**

Young learner version:

```text
Find the force arrows.
Add the force arrows.
The result is the net force.

But:
do not invent force arrows just because they add to the answer.
```

---

## 1. Where This Lesson Begins

In NML2.1, students were allowed to receive a `net-force` arrow as a supplied quantity.

They could use:

```text
mass + net force -> acceleration
```

but they were not yet told where the net-force arrow came from.

NML3 opens that box.

The new question is:

> **If several physical interactions act on one object, how do they combine into the one net-force vector used by Newton II?**

The first answer is familiar:

> **Forces are vectors, so we add them as vectors.**

The deeper answer comes at the end of the lesson:

> **The terms in the force sum are not chosen freely. They represent the particular force contributions associated with the object's physical interactions.**

---

## 2. Learning Objectives

By the end of the lesson, students should be able to:

1. Recognise a force as a vector represented by an arrow.
2. Read the direction and relative size of a force arrow.
3. Add forces acting in the same direction.
4. Combine forces acting in opposite directions.
5. Add forces graphically when they point in different directions.
6. Identify the vector sum as the **net force** on the selected object.
7. Understand that zero net force can result from cancelling nonzero forces.
8. Keep forces attached to one selected target object.
9. Distinguish **adding known forces** from **inventing a decomposition of the net-force vector**.
10. State that the physically relevant force sum is the one whose terms represent the interactions the object is actually experiencing.

---

## 3. The Main Student Route

```text
choose the target object
    -> identify the force arrows acting on that object
    -> keep each force as a separate arrow
    -> add the arrows as vectors
    -> obtain one resultant arrow
    -> call that resultant the net force
```

End-of-lesson refinement:

```text
actual interaction forces
    -> force arrows
    -> vector addition
    -> net force

NOT:

net force
    -> any convenient decomposition
    -> pretend those pieces are physical forces
```

A short classroom rule:

> **Forces first. Addition second.**

---

# Part I — Forces as Arrows

## 4. Force Has Size and Direction

Begin with the ordinary vector picture.

A force arrow tells us two things:

```text
arrow length      -> size of the force
arrow direction   -> direction of the force
```

For example:

```text
5 N right  ->

3 N left   <-
```

The arrow is not the object itself. It represents one force acting on the selected object.

### Teacher wording

> A force is not just a number. A force points somewhere. That is why we draw it as an arrow.

### Quick activity

Give students cards such as:

```text
2 N right
5 N left
4 N upward
3 N downward
```

Ask them to draw one arrow for each.

---

# Part II — Adding Forces in One Dimension

## 5. Forces in the Same Direction

Example:

```text
4 N right + 3 N right
```

Both arrows point the same way, so their sizes add:

$$
4\text{ N}+3\text{ N}=7\text{ N}.
$$

Result:

```text
7 N right
```

Teacher line:

> When the arrows point the same way, the combined arrow gets longer.

---

## 6. Forces in Opposite Directions

Example:

```text
7 N right
5 N left
```

Choose right as positive:

$$
+7\text{ N}+(-5\text{ N})=+2\text{ N}.
$$

Result:

```text
2 N right
```

Young learner version:

> The two arrows partly cancel. The stronger direction wins by the difference.

### Important example: complete cancellation

```text
5 N right
5 N left
```

The vector sum is:

$$
\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}=\mathbf 0.
$$

But there were still two force arrows in the account.

Write prominently:

```text
zero net force
DOES NOT MEAN
no forces were present
```

This should be installed before the deeper force-account discussion.

---

# Part III — Adding Forces in More Than One Direction

## 7. Head-to-Tail Vector Addition

Now use two forces that are not on the same line.

Example:

```text
4 N right
3 N up
```

Draw the first force arrow.

Move a copy of the second arrow so that its tail begins at the head of the first.

Then draw the resultant from the beginning of the first arrow to the end of the second.

```text
       ↑ 3 N
       |
       |     resultant
       |    /
       |   /
-------+--/---->
       4 N
```

For students ready for Pythagoras:

$$
|\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}|
=
\sqrt{4^2+3^2}
=
5\text{ N}.
$$

The important idea is not the triangle formula. It is:

> **Different force arrows can be replaced, for the purpose of Newton II, by one resultant arrow equal to their vector sum.**

---

## 8. Order Does Not Change the Result

Show:

```text
right then up
```

and:

```text
up then right
```

The same resultant appears.

Student conclusion:

```text
F1 + F2 = F2 + F1
```

Do not yet turn this into a formal set-theory lesson. The practical message is:

> We are combining the force arrows, not describing a sequence in which the forces happen.

Several forces may act at the same time.

---

# Part IV — The Net Force

## 9. Many Force Arrows In, One Net-Force Arrow Out

Introduce the main compression:

```text
force 1
force 2
force 3
...
    -> add as vectors
    -> net force
```

For three supplied force vectors:

$$
\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}
=
\mathbf F_1+
\mathbf F_2+
\mathbf F_3.
$$

Teacher wording:

> The net force is the one arrow that has the same vector effect, in Newton II, as all the force arrows added together.

Connection back to NML2.1:

```text
NML3:
force arrows -> net force

NML2.1:
net force + mass -> acceleration
```

Together:

```text
physical interactions
    -> force contributions
    -> vector sum
    -> net force
    -> Newton II
    -> acceleration
```

---

## 10. Target Discipline

Before adding, ask:

> **Which object are these forces acting on?**

Do not mix force arrows belonging to different targets.

Example:

```text
force of the table on the book
force of the book on the table
```

Even if the arrows happen to have related sizes, they do not automatically belong in the same force sum because they act on different target objects.

At this level, keep the rule simple:

> **One force sum belongs to one target object.**

Do not yet teach the full action-reaction structure.

---

# Part V — Cancellation Does Not Erase the Forces

## 11. Balanced Forces Still Belong to the Account

Suppose the selected object has:

```text
6 N right
6 N left
```

Then:

$$
\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}=\mathbf 0.
$$

But the force account still contains two force contributions.

This gives the student two different pictures:

```text
CASE A
no force contributions
-> net force = 0

CASE B
6 N right + 6 N left
-> net force = 0
```

The output vector is the same.

The force accounts are not the same.

Teacher line:

> The sum tells you the final arrow. It does not always tell you what arrows were added to make it.

This is the bridge into the distinctive NML3 ending.

---

# Part VI — The Decomposition Trap

## 12. One Vector Can Be Split in Many Ways

Write one net-force arrow:

```text
10 N right
```

Ask students for ways of writing 10 as a sum.

They may produce:

$$
10=6+4,
$$

$$
10=5+5,
$$

$$
10=20-10.
$$

Therefore the vector can be written as:

$$
10\text{ N right}
=
6\text{ N right}+4\text{ N right},
$$

or

$$
10\text{ N right}
=
5\text{ N right}+5\text{ N right},
$$

or even

$$
10\text{ N right}
=
20\text{ N right}+10\text{ N left}.
$$

All of these are correct vector arithmetic.

Now ask:

> Does that mean the object was really experiencing two forces of 5 N?

Expected answer:

> No.

Ask:

> Does the last equation prove that there really was a 20 N force right and a 10 N force left?

Expected answer:

> No.

### Central distinction

```text
A vector decomposition tells us
how one vector can be written as a sum.

A physical force account tells us
which force contributions actually belong to the object.
```

The first is mathematics.

The second is a claim about the physical situation.

---

## 13. The Physically Relevant Decomposition

This is the distinctive end-point of the lesson.

Many decompositions of a net-force vector are mathematically possible.

But Newtonian force accounting singles out a particular collection of force contributions:

> **the force contributions that represent the physical interactions the selected object is actually experiencing.**

Student-facing version:

> You are allowed to split a vector in many mathematical ways. But you are not allowed to turn every mathematical piece into a new physical force.

Another useful sentence:

> **The correct force sum represents the object's interactions; it is not merely any sum that reaches the correct resultant.**

Possible board formulation:

```text
MATHEMATICS:
Many different sets of arrows can add to the same resultant.

PHYSICS:
The force account is supposed to contain the arrows that represent
what is actually interacting with the object.
```

Avoid saying at this age that students already know a complete experimental procedure for proving every interaction. That belongs to later NML3 / acting-object work.

The point here is only to install the distinction:

```text
adds correctly
≠
belongs physically
```

---

# Part VII — A Simple Interaction Example

## 14. Box on a Table

Consider a box resting on a table.

For the present lesson, **supply** the two physical interactions rather than asking students to derive them:

```text
Earth interacts with the box.
The table interacts with the box.
```

Represent the resulting force contributions as:

```text
Earth contribution: 5 N down
Table contribution: 5 N up
```

Then:

$$
\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}
=
5\text{ N up}+5\text{ N down}
=
\mathbf 0.
$$

Now compare with an arbitrary decomposition:

$$
\mathbf 0
=
20\text{ N right}+20\text{ N left}.
$$

Ask:

> Is that equation mathematically true?

Yes.

> Does it show that there is really a 20 N rightward interaction and a 20 N leftward interaction on the box?

No.

Why not?

> Because those arrows were invented from the resultant. They were not supplied as force contributions representing interactions on the box.

This gives the student the correct direction of reasoning:

```text
physical interactions
    -> force contributions
    -> add them
    -> net force
```

not:

```text
net force
    -> invent pieces
    -> call the pieces interactions
```

---

# Part VIII — Lesson Summary

## 15. The Four Rules

End with four rules on the board.

### Rule 1 — Forces are vectors

```text
A force has size and direction.
```

### Rule 2 — Add the forces on one target

```text
F_net = F1 + F2 + ...
```

### Rule 3 — Cancellation happens in the sum

```text
nonzero forces can add to zero
```

### Rule 4 — Do not invent forces from the answer

```text
Many vector decompositions are possible.
Only the physical force account is meant to represent the object's interactions.
```

Shortest version:

> **Find the forces. Then add them. Do not find the sum and invent the forces afterward.**

---

# Part IX — Exit Questions

## 16. Exit Ticket

### Question 1

A toy car has a 7 N force to the right and a 2 N force to the left.
What is the net force?

Expected:

```text
5 N right
```

### Question 2

Two forces act on one object:

```text
4 N right
3 N up
```

Draw the resultant using head-to-tail addition.

### Question 3

An object has two equal and opposite force contributions.
Its net force is zero.
Does that prove that no forces act on it?

Expected:

```text
No. Nonzero force contributions can cancel.
```

### Question 4

A measured net-force vector is 10 N right.
A student writes:

```text
5 N right + 5 N right = 10 N right
```

Has the student shown that two 5 N forces physically act on the object?

Expected:

```text
No. They have shown only one possible vector decomposition.
```

### Question 5 — Signature NML3 question

Why can we not choose any arrows that add to the net force and call them the forces on the object?

Target answer:

> Because vector addition only tells us which arrows give the same resultant. The physical force account is supposed to contain the force contributions that represent the object's actual interactions.

Strong answer:

> Many decompositions of the same net-force vector are mathematically possible. The physically relevant decomposition is the one built from the actual force contributions on the target object, and those contributions are identified before they are added.

# Part X — Teacher Notes

## 17. What Is Conventional Here

Most of the lesson should feel like an ordinary introductory forces lesson:

```text
force arrows
vector direction and magnitude
same-direction addition
opposite-direction cancellation
head-to-tail vector addition
resultant/net force
balanced forces
```

That is intentional.

NML3 does not need to make ordinary vector addition seem strange.

The conceptual extension arrives after students already understand the sum.

---

## 18. What Is Distinctive Here

The distinctive NML3 move is to refuse this hidden textbook step:

```text
Here is the net force.
Now decompose it into whatever named forces make the equation work.
```

Instead, the lesson installs:

```text
interaction account first
sum second
```

At younger levels, this can remain mostly verbal.

At higher levels, the same idea will be sharpened using:

```text
interacting-forces-set
force-member identity
acting-object-instance keys
set uniqueness
equal vectors from distinct members
empty versus nonempty zero-resultant accounts
generation versus closure
```

The younger lesson should prepare for those ideas without requiring their formal machinery.

---

## 19. Vocabulary Choice

For younger students, prefer:

```text
force contribution
force arrow
force account
physical interaction
represents the interaction
```

The verb **represents** is probably the cleanest choice here.

Possible teacher sentence:

> **The force account is the collection of force contributions used to represent the physical interactions experienced by the target object.**

`Captures` is intuitive but slightly metaphorical.

`Appropriates` sounds too legal/technical and may suggest ownership rather than representation.

`Represents` leaves room for the important later distinction between:

```text
physical interaction
force-instance
vector representation of that force-instance
```

---

## 20. Boundaries

Do not yet require students to solve:

```text
How do we prove every candidate interaction is real?
How do we know we have found every interaction?
How are force members keyed?
What makes one force an action and another a reaction?
How do concrete acting objects generate their force laws?
```

Those are later parts of the NML3 / Newton sequence.

For this younger lesson it is enough that supplied or clearly established force contributions are treated as prior to the vector sum.

The installed direction should be:

```text
forces first
sum second
```

---

## 21. Final Board

```text
ADDING FORCES

1. Choose one target object.
2. Identify the force contributions on it.
3. Draw each as a vector.
4. Add the vectors.
5. The resultant is the net force.

Important:
Many different vector decompositions can give the same resultant.
That does not make every decomposition a real force account.

The physical force account is the one meant to represent
what is actually interacting with the object.

FORCES FIRST. ADDITION SECOND.
```
