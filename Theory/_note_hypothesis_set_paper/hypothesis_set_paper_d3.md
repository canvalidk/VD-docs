# Where does the theory say to look? Hypothesis sets and the delay before belief moves

*Alternative titles:* (a) The patch of sky: what a failed prediction is a failure of. (b) Undecided: hypothesis sets and the window before belief moves.

**Can Valid**

**Abstract.** A theory never predicts a bare outcome. When a prediction p is derived from a theory T together with commitments A₁, …, Aₙ, what T entails is the disjunction p ∨ ¬A₁ ∨ … ∨ ¬Aₙ. I call that disjunction the hypothesis set. Its logical kernel is old. What has not been done is to treat the disjunction as an object fixed before the test, whose members are decided one by one by ordinary means, with belief in T unmoved until the last member is rejected. I set this out, retell Neptune and Vulcan in its terms, and draw out a mechanism for the delay between an anomaly and any movement of belief: a window as long as the slowest member, filled by the member tests, lengthened by every mistake found on the way. I end with a problem: for Newtonian mechanics, or any major theory, what is the set?

## 1. The patch of sky

On the night of 23–24 September 1846, Johann Galle and Heinrich d'Arrest at the Berlin observatory turned a telescope on a place in the sky that Urbain Le Verrier had written down for them, and found a planet. The place had come from Uranus, which for some twenty years had been falling behind the tables computed for it, and from a calculation. If Newton's law held, and if the Sun and the known planets were the only bodies acting, the tables should have fitted; they did not; so Le Verrier asked where one further Newtonian body would have to be to produce the departure, and in three memoirs to the Académie des sciences over the ten months to August 1846 he answered (Le Verrier 1845; 1846a; 1846b). Neptune was found under a degree from the predicted longitude.[^1] Thirteen years later he did the same thing again. In 1859 he reported that Mercury's perihelion was advancing some 38 arcseconds a century faster than the Sun and the known planets could account for, and proposed that matter inside Mercury's orbit, a planet or a ring of small bodies, was responsible (Le Verrier 1859). The searches ran for half a century and found nothing; in 1909 W. W. Campbell declared the observational side of the question closed (Campbell 1909). In November 1915 Einstein derived 43 arcseconds a century from general relativity with nothing to adjust (Einstein 1915), and the anomaly has been his ever since.

The two episodes are a textbook pair, and I do not claim them as a discovery. Hanson (1962) built an article on them as the zenith and nadir of Newtonian mechanics; Hempel (1966, pp. 52, 54) set them side by side; Gillies (1993, pp. 98–116) states them in exactly the logical form I will use, two anomalies of the same prima facie kind, one resolved by changing an auxiliary hypothesis and the other by changing the main theory; Lahav and Massimi (2014) call them the battleground of twentieth-century methodology. What interests me is a question the pair makes visible and the literature has not, so far as I can find, asked in its own right. In both cases astronomers had to go and look before Newton could be judged. That was not a choice: until the patch of sky had been examined, the departure of the orbit was evidence of nothing in particular. Yet nowhere in Newton's theory is the demand stated. Nothing in the law of gravitation or the laws of motion says: if a computed orbit is off, compute where one further body would have to be and examine that region, because if something is there the theory stands, and if nothing is there the theory may be in trouble. The demand is real, it was obeyed twice, and it has no address.

This paper does three things. First, it assembles the answer the literature already half-contains: that what a theory entails, when a prediction is derived through commitments, is not the prediction but a disjunction, which I call the hypothesis set,[^2] and that an observation counts for or against the theory only through the fate of that disjunction's members. Second, it draws out one consequence of taking the set seriously: a mechanism for the delay between an anomaly and any movement of belief, whose length and content the set determines. Third, it turns the opening question into a problem that can be worked on: for a given theory, what is the set, and where does it come from?

Section 2 reviews what Duhem, Quine, Popper, Lakatos, Kuhn and the Bayesian and error-statistical traditions already say, and names the gap. Sections 3 to 5 state the set, the delay and the attitude that reproducible belief produces. Section 6 poses the problem.

## 2. What the literature already says

The logical kernel is old. Duhem (1954 [1906], p. 187) argued that an experiment in physics never condemns one hypothesis on its own but bears on a group of hypotheses together, and does not say which member of the group to change. Quine (1951, pp. 39–40) generalised it: any statement can be "held true come what may" if enough is adjusted elsewhere, and the unit that faces experience is the whole of science. Between the two sits the textbook schema (Hempel 1966, p. 23; Ladyman 2002, pp. 77–81): if H with auxiliary hypotheses A entails I, and I is false, then H or A or both are false, so a failed test is no conclusive ground for rejecting H alone. Gillies (1993) attaches Neptune and Vulcan to it. The box in Section 3 is this kernel, written as a disjunction that the theory itself entails before anything has failed.

Popper treated the kernel as a danger to be legislated against. Sections 19 and 20 of *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (1959, pp. 60–62) list the conventionalist stratagems, among them ad hoc auxiliary hypotheses, and rule that an auxiliary may be admitted only if it does not reduce the falsifiability of the system. Popper read Neptune as I do: in that book it is a model of theory-guided discovery (p. 90 n. 2), and in *Conjectures and Refutations* (1963, p. 220) Galle's discovery heads a list of corroborations by severe tests. The often repeated claim that he later called the Neptune hypothesis a shelter for Newton's theory rests on a page of his replies to his critics (Popper 1974, p. 986) that I have not seen and cite through Bamford (1996) and Lahav and Massimi (2014) [check]. The worry behind the rule is every reader of Duhem's: if the auxiliaries can always be repaired, the theory risks nothing.

Lakatos (1970, pp. 100–101) answered that worry with a parable, told against dogmatic falsificationism, in which a Newtonian physicist meets a deviating planet with an unseen planet, then a dust cloud, then a magnetic field, each tested and found wanting, and never recognises a refutation. The story is Neptune and Vulcan as one parable, though the essay never names either; its moral is that appraisal belongs to research programmes, judged degenerating only in hindsight (pp. 120, 174). Two other things in the essay matter more here. At pp. 110–111 Lakatos has his methodological falsificationist decide the conjuncts of a refuted test one after another: Mercury's perihelion first refutes a conjunction of theory, initial conditions and a ceteris paribus clause; the initial conditions are tested severely and relegated to background knowledge; the ceteris paribus clause is tested by specifying and refuting interfering factors; only when it too is accepted does the anomaly count as a falsification. That is the closest prior statement I have found of members decided one by one, and one of the set's members is, as Section 3 concedes, Lakatos's clause under another name. The differences are two. The members of the set are read from the derivation rather than supplied by the defender, so the kind of thing that may be blamed, and the kind of test that decides it, are fixed before the anomaly; and Lakatos deliberately says nothing about belief, which his methodology excludes from appraisal, whereas I say what belief does while the deciding goes on. The other thing is a number: at p. 115 he observes that eighty-five years passed between Mercury's perihelion being accepted as an anomaly and as a falsification. That is the delay of my title, measured, though the end date is his own. Schurz's non-monotonic reconstruction of the Neptune case (2001, as Zenker 2006 reports it) [check] formalises the repair, the addition of an auxiliary after which the false prediction no longer follows, and not the verdict.

Kuhn (1962, chs VI–VIII) supplies the phenomenology. Anomaly need not produce crisis, and scientists do not renounce a paradigm over a persistent discrepancy; his example (p. 81) is that Newtonian theory was never seriously questioned over Mercury, whose misbehaviour vanished with general relativity after a crisis it had done nothing to bring about. I will call the period during which an anomaly is undecided the window. Kuhn's crisis, when there is one, falls inside it; Mercury shows that a window can run for half a century with no crisis at all, and Section 4 says why that is expected.

The Bayesian treatments of Duhem's problem are the main rival to the account I give. Dorling (1979) reconstructed the case of the Moon's secular acceleration with a prior of 0.9 on the theory and 0.6 on the auxiliary and found, after the anomaly, the theory at 0.897 and the auxiliary at 0.003, as Mayo (1996, pp. 111–112; 1997, p. 227) reports the calculation; Howson and Urbach (2006, pp. 109–113) do the same for Prout's hypothesis; Fitelson and Waterman (2005) and Earman (1992, pp. 75, 83–86) dispute the apportioning; Gershman (2019) and Costa (2025) use Neptune and Vulcan to motivate treatments of this kind. The tradition apportions blame between theory and auxiliaries by priors and likelihoods, and belief in the theory moves at once, by a small amount. The standing objection, which Earman (p. 85) concedes in calling the result a highly qualified success, is that the apportioning depends on priors on the auxiliaries, and on a likelihood for the anomaly under the theory's unspecified rivals, that nobody has. The Bayesian result nearest to mine is Strevens's (2001): when the auxiliaries concern the conditions of the experiment, the theory's posterior barely moves after a failed prediction. Strevens gets a small movement from a restriction on the auxiliaries; the hypothesis set gets no movement at all, from the structure of the verdict, without that restriction. The two lineages thus agree on the phenomenon and differ on the mechanism: a small immediate movement governed by a prior nobody has, against no movement until a member is decided.

Two further pieces of the literature come close. Glymour's bootstrapping (1980, p. 127), in which theory and data together fix the quantities that test a hypothesis, is the nearest thing I know to the claim that the record of residuals fixes where to look. Mayo's error statistics is nearer still. Her piecemeal approach (1996, ch. 4; 1997, §4.1) splits the testing of auxiliaries from the appraisal of the primary hypothesis and blocks the blaming of an anomaly on an auxiliary unless the auxiliary's falsity has itself passed a severe test, one that would probably have found the claim false had it been false (1997, p. 232; 2018, p. 23). I lean on severity in Section 4. Nor is a pre-fixed list of candidates for blame foreign to her: the hierarchy of primary, experimental and data models (1997, §4) is such a list, and so are her canonical types of error (1996) [check]. The difference is that her list is generic, the same for every experiment, whereas the hypothesis set is specific to one derivation and contains search members, whose content comes from the theory and the record and which no generic list can hold; and that I ask what belief does while the list is worked through, a question her title of 1997 declines. Kelly's learning theory (1996) measures inquiry by its retractions; Kelly (2000) derives features of Kuhnian historiography from the logic of convergence, and his later work on Ockham efficiency argues that retraction-minimising inquiry keeps its current hypothesis until forced to retract [check]. That is a result about the shape of the delay, not its content.

Here is the gap. Popper's independently testable auxiliaries and Mayo's experimental assumptions are specified before the test; the Bayesian holds the auxiliaries in a prior; Lakatos decides them in series. None treats the disjunction itself as one object with a state, each member held, rejected or open, that fixes what the trial has so far said about the theory, with belief unchanged until closure. That object is the hypothesis set, and the rest of this paper is about what follows from naming it.

## 3. The hypothesis set

Let a prediction p be derived from a theory T together with commitments A₁, …, Aₙ. I use "commitment" for what Duhem and Quine call an auxiliary hypothesis, because I mean the auxiliaries as they appear in an explicit derivation: the frame is inertial, the force account is complete, the instrument was read correctly, the arithmetic was right, this approximation holds here. Then:

> **Definition.** Given a correct derivation T ∧ A₁ ∧ … ∧ Aₙ ⊢ p, the **hypothesis set** of the derivation is H = {p, ¬A₁, …, ¬Aₙ}. Each member of H is a hypothesis with its own test, and at any time it is *held*, *rejected* or *open*.
>
> **Verdict rule.** The trial *counts for T* as soon as any member of H is held; it *counts against T* when every member of H has been rejected; otherwise it is *undecided*.

T entails the disjunction of H by the deduction theorem and De Morgan: T ⊢ (A₁ ∧ … ∧ Aₙ) → p, so T ⊢ p ∨ ¬A₁ ∨ … ∨ ¬Aₙ. One qualification. H has members of two sorts: the negations of the premises Aᵢ, which are disjuncts of what T entails exactly as written, and the claims that the derivation was correctly carried out, which condition the entailment itself, since a derivation with a slip in it is a derivation of something else. I treat the second sort as premises all the same, because the field tests them as hypotheses, by recomputation, and because what happens when one holds is, as Section 4 shows, a large part of the delay. So the set is not something added to the theory to protect it. It is what the theory says about the situation once the commitments are set beside it, and it is all the theory says about this trial.

Notice first what is not a member. "T fails" is not in H. It is not a disjunct of anything T entails and not a hypothesis anyone tests; it is the name of the state in which every member has been rejected. This answers the objection that a disjunction with an escape clause covers every outcome and so predicts nothing. The set excludes exactly one region of possibility, ¬p ∧ A₁ ∧ … ∧ Aₙ, in which the point prediction fails and every commitment holds, and that region is T's empirical content for the situation. A trial that lands there counts against T; everything else is a hit or an open question about which commitment gave way.

Notice next what a member is. For a Newtonian derivation of a planet's position the set reads, in prose: the body is at the predicted place at the predicted time; or there is a mistake in the calculation; or the inputs were wrong, because the record is unfaithful to what was seen or because it was misread; or the frame is not inertial; or the account of the forces is incomplete, some body acting on the system having been left out; or an approximation broke down; and whatever else the derivation used, which is a finite list once the derivation is on the page. "Somebody made a mistake" is not one member but a family, one definite claim for each step at which a mistake could have been made. Each member is tested by ordinary means: a suspected mistake by recomputation, a suspected input by re-measurement and inspection of the apparatus, a suspected missing body by a search, the whole trial by an independent rerun. Each test has its own significance level and power, and nothing I say requires the levels to agree. "Held" means the test came out in the member's favour, the mistake found or the body seen; "rejected" that it came out against, the recomputation agreeing or the search coming up empty at its detection limit.

One member on that list is not like the others. "The account of the forces is incomplete" is existentially quantified: some body was left out, it says, without saying which, and no finite test rejects an existential claim outright. It is Lakatos's ceteris paribus clause, read off the derivation instead of supplied by the defender. The derivation adds the form of the test, since it fixes which forces were counted and so what an omitted one would have to do to the residuals; the test adds a bound. A search member is rejected only by exhaustion relative to a detection limit, no body brighter than such a magnitude within so many degrees of the Sun, so its rejection is bounded to a domain and a precision, and so is any verdict against T that rests on it. That is the sense in which the set lists finitely many definite members: finitely many kinds, each with a definite test, one of which closes only up to a limit the field states.

Notice last that the set is fixed before the test, which is where the account departs from Section 2. The commitments in H are the ones actually used in deriving p; anyone who writes the derivation out in full can list them in advance, and the list does not change when the observation comes in. Writing derivations out that fully is unusual in physics, where a competent reader supplies the commitments silently, and I think that is one reason the set has gone unnamed: it is never on the page, so it never looks like an object. In two cases its members were worked through in public.

Neptune, properly told, is not a failed prediction that was rescued. Newton never predicted the bare position of Uranus. Bouvard's tables of 1821 rested on Newton's law together with the commitment that the forces on Uranus are those of the Sun and the known planets (Alexander 1970); so the set contained the member "or the force account is incomplete", and when the planet had fallen some two minutes of arc behind the tables by the mid-1840s, that member was open together with the others. Le Verrier closed the others first. His memoirs of November 1845 and June 1846 recomputed the perturbations by Jupiter and Saturn, found that corrections to Uranus's own elements could absorb only a minor part of the departure, declared the observations incompatible with the known planets alone,[^3] and set aside a resisting medium, a satellite, a comet and a planet inside Uranus's orbit, each a definite version of the open member, and an altered law of gravitation, which is a rival to Newton rather than a member of its set. What remained was the version of the member whose test was a search, and the test was the memoir of 31 August 1846: the single Newtonian body that reproduces the residuals at every observed moment, with its longitude, distance and mass stated (Le Verrier 1846b). Galle and d'Arrest found it, under a degree from the prediction. The member held. The trial counted for Newton. It only looked like a failure, and then like a rescue, to a reading of Newton without the set.

Where the member's content came from bears on the objection that an unseen body can absorb anything. What made the member definite was the record. Uranus and Neptune had passed heliocentric conjunction around 1821–22; before it Neptune hurried Uranus along its orbit, after it held it back. Tables fitted to the 1781–1820 arc could represent that arc without any extra force, so the departure appeared only as a lag growing from the mid-1820s; the reversal shows only against a model of the unperturbed orbit. One body, obeying the same law, had to produce the residual at every one of those moments at once, and on my reading of the figures the constraint was tight in one respect and loose in another. The direction was constrained, up to the ambiguity that Lai, Lam and Young (1990) find in the inverse problem, a second solution diametrically opposite the first. The distance was not: Neptune's semi-major axis is about 30 astronomical units and its mass about one 19,000th of the Sun's, outside Le Verrier's stated limits, which is why Benjamin Peirce called the discovery a happy accident (Peirce 1847).[^1] The theory supplied the member's form, a Newtonian body; the record supplied its content; and what the record constrained is what the search tested.

Vulcan used the same member, licensed by the same theory and tested by the same kind of search, and it has to be told to its actual end. Le Verrier's letter to Faye, read to the Académie on 12 September 1859, reported that the transits of Mercury could be represented only once some 38 arcseconds a century were added to the secular motion of the perihelion; Newcomb's revision of 1882 made it nearly 43. From the start Le Verrier allowed either a single planet or a ring of small bodies, and gave his reasons: a planet of the needed mass would have been seen at eclipses or in transit; a ring's periodic actions cancel while its secular action adds up; and in any case the secular term fixes only a relation between the perturber's mass and its distance. That last remark is the difference from Uranus. By Gauss's theorem a perturbing mass spread along its orbit produces, to first order in that mass, the same secular perturbation as a body moving on the orbit; what distinguishes a body from a ring, and one distance from another, lives in periodic terms, and for Mercury those were too small to detect.[^4] So the member had almost no content that pointed anywhere, and each definite version of it had to be tried separately (Fontenrose 1973; Roseveare 1982).

The planet was searched for at transits and eclipses for fifty years and never found; the sightings claimed along the way were unmade one by one, and Campbell declared the observational side closed in 1909 (Campbell 1909). The ring was rejected by Newcomb (1882; 1895) on visibility, on stability, and on the motion of Mercury's node, which a ring in the ecliptic would have disturbed as much as the perihelion and a flattened disc would have moved the wrong way. The figure of the Sun was rejected when Auwers's heliometer measures showed nothing near the half-second of ellipticity required (Newcomb 1895, §56). Hall's altered exponent of 1894 was not a member of Newton's set but a rival theory, and the motion of the Moon killed it in 1903 (Hall 1894; Brown 1903). One member was still open in 1915. Seeliger's zodiacal-light matter of 1906, with a total mass near 3 × 10⁻⁷ of the Sun's, fitted all of Newcomb's residual terms (Seeliger 1906), was under active examination through 1915 and was still cited beside Einstein in 1923.[^5] When general relativity produced 43 arcseconds a century with no adjustable quantity, the community treated the trial as decided against Newton. The rule as I have stated it does not say so: under the rule the trial was still undecided in 1915, and the verdict was an inference the community drew outside it, which Section 4 takes up. What the rule does say is that any verdict against Newton here is bounded to that class of situation at that precision, and at the precision of 1915 nothing else in Newton's record moves.

One more point before the delay. No free parameter is charged to the theory. The member is not "some body with a fitted mass and orbit"; it is the claim that a Newtonian body exists which produces these residuals, and Newton together with the record fixes what its test must be. The test needed the one quantity the record did constrain, the direction; the mass and distance Le Verrier also published were parameters of the test's design, fixed by an assumption he had to make to run the search [check] and corrected after it, and they belong to the member's test, not to T. The calculation was the test of the member, not a price paid by the theory. Here my account and the Bayesian one part most visibly: there the rescue carries a prior cost and the theory pays at once; here the calculation costs the theory nothing, because nothing about the theory has yet been decided, and the calculation is how it gets decided.

## 4. The delay

Now suppose that belief in T is kept as a tally. For each class of situations, a cart on a track, a planet's orbit, a muon in a storage ring, keep a count of the trials that have counted for T and of those that have counted against it, and let belief that T is reliable on the class be Bayesian over the count. A class is a triple: a type of situation, a precision at which the point prediction is held to, and a significance level for its test. The choice is conventional and is made by the field that keeps the tally; the account does not solve the reference-class problem (Reichenbach 1949, §85), but it makes the problem visible where it sits. I will picture the tally as an urn. A trial that counts for T drops a green ball into the urn for its class; a trial that counts against T drops a red one; belief is the Bayesian estimate of the proportion of green among the draws to come. An undecided trial is a ball still in hand. It has no colour and carries no weight. That last clause is the whole mechanism. It is also a deliberate departure from the probability calculus, which should be owned: the model has two levels, members receiving categorical verdicts by the field's own tests at the field's own levels, Bayes applying only to the tally of decided trials, and an undecided trial being given no likelihood at all. A Bayesian will say that if the point test has any severity, ¬p already lowers the probability of T whatever the members' state; the tally instead takes the verdicts the field delivers and does not interpolate between them, for reasons I give in a note.[^6]

The first consequence is that belief does not move during the window. When the point prediction is rejected and the other members are open, the trial is undecided, the ball is in hand, and the tally is exactly what it was. Nothing has happened to belief in T, not because anyone has decided to protect T but because nothing has yet been said about it. What happens instead is the member tests: recomputation (the mistake members), inspection of the instruments and re-measurement of the inputs (the input members), searches (the missing-object members), and attempts by other groups to reproduce the result, which test the mistake and input members of the original trial and, if the reproduction misses too, open a fresh trial with a set of its own. This is the period working scientists describe in a stock phrase: a major result, everyone goes back through the measurements and the reasoning, others try to reproduce it. The window is not Kuhn's crisis. Crisis is one thing that may fill a window, as theory preprints filled OPERA's; member tests are another, and Vulcan's window ran for half a century of searches with no crisis, which is what Kuhn's remark about Mercury records and what the account predicts when open members are being worked through routinely. Duhem's good sense, which he said ends the indecision between rival responses to an anomaly where logic cannot (1954, pp. 216–218), here has a job description: the indecision ends when the last open member is decided. The formal models of scientific delay I know of (Zollman 2007, 2010; Weisberg and Muldoon 2009; O'Connor and Weatherall 2018) work at the level of the community and have every agent update on every datum; none models the closing of members, and the two kinds of delay are distinct, one about how agents share evidence, the other about when there is any evidence to share.

The second consequence is that the window lasts as long as the slowest member. A trial can count against T only when every member has been rejected, so the time to a verdict against T is the time to close the last one. More to close, longer window: a theory whose predictions depend on more independently testable commitments is slower to reject, and this is arithmetic, not attachment. What counts is independent member tests, not members as written: splitting "the inputs were right" into ten members does not lengthen a window if one re-measurement closes all ten, and at Vulcan one member, "the forces are complete", hid a dozen separate searches. The granularity of a set is fixed by the tests available, not by how finely the derivation is written, and the account needs the corresponding invariance: two derivations of the same prediction that differ only in how the commitments are grouped have the same window. With that understood, if the member tests run in parallel at comparable rates the expected time to close them all grows like the logarithm of their number; one after another, like the number itself.[^7] Established theories fail slowly because closing the set takes time. Vulcan's window was set by its slowest member, and the slowest was not the eclipse search, which closed by 1909, but the diffuse-matter member, for which no search could be designed and which was still open when a rival produced the figure.

The third consequence is the one I think has been missed, and it concerns mistakes. The set said "or somebody made a mistake here". When a mistake is found, that member holds, and by the verdict rule the trial counts for T: the prediction as stated, which was a disjunction, was right. The ball is green and goes into the urn. But nothing stops anyone from correcting the derivation and redoing the experiment, and everyone does. That is a new trial, with its own point prediction and its own set, and if its point prediction misses too, its own window runs. So a real anomaly first blamed on a mistake reaches the theory one rerun and one whole window later than it otherwise would, and each further mistake found along the way adds another rerun and another window. The intermediate position should be stated plainly, because it is counter-intuitive and it is what the rule implies: during the rerun window the theory has gained a trial in its favour, and nothing yet counts against it. The tally for T stands one higher than before the anomaly was announced.

OPERA is the worked case. On 22 September 2011 the OPERA collaboration posted a preprint, presented at CERN the next day, reporting that muon neutrinos sent from CERN to Gran Sasso had arrived about 60 nanoseconds earlier than light would have, a six-standard-deviation effect on 16,111 events (Adam et al. 2011).[^8] The point prediction of special relativity had been rejected beyond the field's own threshold for discovery.[^9] The ball was in hand, and the record of the following months is a record of member tests. The collaboration had spent months looking for an instrumental cause and offered no interpretation (CERN 2011); named physicists said within days that they expected an error to be found, without being able to say where; theorists argued that superluminal neutrinos would have lost energy on the way (Cohen and Glashow 2011); and between 22 October and 6 November the collaboration reran the measurement with short proton bunches and found the same early arrival, which removed one family of suspected mistakes, those in the treatment of the long proton pulses, and left the rest open. On 22 February 2012 the story leaked (Cartlidge 2012), and on 23 February CERN named two candidate faults of opposite sign, neither yet quantified: a fibre connector to the master clock, not fully seated, and an oscillator running fast. The connector was then quantified, from measurements begun in December 2011, at 73 nanoseconds, by which the reduced light reaching the clock's electronics had shifted its time reference since about August 2008 and made the neutrinos look early; the shift was found independently by timing horizontal muons through the LVD and OPERA detectors (Agafonova et al. 2012); and the member was held when the corrected 2009–2011 data gave an early arrival consistent with zero (Adam et al. 2012). An input member had held; the trial counted for relativity and the ball turned green.

Then came the reruns: ICARUS, an independent detector in the same laboratory, in March 2012 (Antonello et al. 2012); OPERA with the faults corrected in May (Adam et al. 2013); and on 8 June 2012, in Kyoto, Borexino, ICARUS, LVD and OPERA together, all consistent with light, which CERN's research director called what everyone had expected deep down (CERN 2012). Each was a new trial whose point prediction held. Two qualifications are owed. The experimental mainstream's belief did not move, on the evidence of what its members said and did; but most of the dozens of theory preprints written in the window took the effect as real, so the claim holds for the experimentalists and not for everyone (Cartwright 2024). And nobody, so far as Cartwright could find, identified the fault in advance.[^10] The member that held, that the timing inputs were wrong, was on the list from the start; the specific way it held was not, and did not need to be.

The solar-neutrino problem is a window of a different length. Davis, Harmer and Hoffman (1968) reported an upper limit of 3 solar neutrino units from the Homestake chlorine detector against a prediction of 7.5 ± 3 in the same issue (Bahcall, Bahcall and Shaviv 1968), and the rate later settled at about a third of the solar model's. Take as T the standard account of the neutrino, massless and unmixed. The point prediction is the capture rate; the commitments include that the solar model gives the flux and that the detector counts what arrives. Bahcall (2004) later described three classes of explanation, that the solar model was wrong, that the experiment was wrong, and that the neutrino physics was wrong. The first two are members. The third is not: it is what is left when both have been rejected, and Section 3 said it is no member of anything T entails. The experimental member was tested by recovery tests on the extraction chemistry (Davis 2003) and rejected when Kamiokande-II saw the same deficit in real time, with the neutrinos pointing back to the Sun (Hirata et al. 1989; 1990). The solar member was rejected by the gallium experiments of 1991–92, which saw the deficit in the low-energy neutrinos too (Abazov et al. 1991; Anselmann et al. 1992), by the pattern of deficits across energies (Haxton 1995) and by helioseismology, which by 1997 matched the model's sound speeds to a fraction of a per cent (Bahcall et al. 1997). With both members rejected the trial counted against T; the result that settled it in the field's eyes was Sudbury's measurement of all flavours together (Ahmad et al. 2001; 2002), which found the missing electron neutrinos in the other flavours, and by Bahcall's account most physicists were not persuaded until 2001. Something over thirty years in hand (Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences 2015), filled from beginning to end with member tests. The verdict rule is applied per conjunct, and the same trial, read with the solar model as T, counted for it.[^11]

Four caveats, stated and not solved. First, a weak point test counts for T cheaply: if the allowance around the point prediction is so wide that the test could not have missed, a held point member says nothing. Mayo's severity requirement is the standard repair and can be imported as a condition on "held": a member counts as held only if its test would probably have rejected it had it been false. The condition is imposed on the member's test, relative to the member; a reader who imposes it on the trial, relative to T, will want a trial decided by a found mistake to return no ball rather than a green one, and the rerun is where the two readings come apart. Second, the rule treats a member's decision as final, and real decisions get revisited; a mistake wrongly believed found surfaces on the rerun, whose derivation carries the supposed correction as a commitment of its own. Third, members tested on shared apparatus are not independent, which affects the arithmetic of the window and of false verdicts but not the structure. Fourth, and most seriously, Vulcan. In 1915 the diffuse-matter member was still open, and the community nonetheless treated the trial as decided against Newton when a rival produced the figure with nothing to adjust. The rule does not license that; a rival's success rejects no member, so the retelling of Section 3 holds in the rule's own terms up to 1909, and the verdict of 1915 was drawn outside it. The obvious reading, which the framework can accommodate but I have not worked out, is that a rival that predicts the figure changes the test of the remaining member: a mass of 3 × 10⁻⁷ solar masses fitted to the residual now competes with a parameter-free prediction of it, and loses. A cleaner form of the same reading: "no further intra-Mercurial matter" is a commitment of general relativity's own derivation of the 43 arcseconds, so the rival's point hit rejects the Seeliger mass given the rival, and the Newtonian member is closed by the rival's set, not by Newton's. Either way the clause is comparative, a member's status then depends on what rivals exist, and that is a real cost to "fixed before the test", which I leave open. Lakatos's eighty-five years fits either reading.

## 5. Absolutely true, absolutely wrong

Reproducibility now does something specific. If a class of situations can be re-instanced at will, if anyone can set up another cart on another track, then anyone can add to its urn, and the tally can be pushed as far as its holder likes. From a flat prior, n trials for T and none against make the probability that T is reliable on the class, in the sense that trials on it count for T all but a small fraction of the time, rise towards one with every trial; any confidence short of certainty is reached in finitely many trials, and certainty never is.[^12] Belief on a reproducible class is belief on tap.

This, I think, is the structure behind a common way of speaking among physicists about their established theories. They call them absolutely true, in a sense they distinguish from religious or personal conviction and regard as more reliable than either, and if asked what "absolutely" means they do not usually say. The model gives the word one precise sense. "Absolute" names a limit: the direction in which reproduction moves belief, which exists, in that the probability in question tends to one, and which no record of trials ever reaches. Absolute truth is therefore not a property a record of trials could certify; it is what the record approaches when the class is open to anyone and trials are cheap, which is why it is not a state of belief, and why it is reasonable to hold: on a reproducible class, anyone who doubts can draw again. What the model calls the absolute truth of T on a class is reliability to within a stated fraction on that class, a domain-restricted and more modest thing than truth. The antecedents are Peirce's fated opinion (1878, §IV), whose own example, the methods of measuring the velocity of light converging on one value, is this per-class object rather than the truth simpliciter he defined by it, and Popper's inter-subjective testability (1959, §8, pp. 22–24), which refuses scientific standing to effects nobody can regularly reproduce. The standing objection is Collins's (1985) experimenters' regress: whether a trial counts as a competent reproduction is itself contested. I accept it as a statement about how "held" and "rejected" get decided in practice; it bears on the member tests, not on the tally.

The mirror image holds in exactly the same sense. Outside its domain a theory can be absolutely wrong. Muon lifetimes in a storage ring and clocks flown around the Earth can be re-run at will, and every trial of Newtonian absolute time on those classes comes out against. The set of such a trial is small, because the prediction is kinematic: that the decaying particle is a clock, that its speed and its count were read correctly, that the frame is inertial to the needed precision, and no search member, since no missing force enters the derivation. Those members are closed by the standing calibration of the apparatus, the same way on every run, so each look at time dilation is a fresh trial against absolute time, and the probability that it is unreliable on the class approaches one in the same way. Both attitudes are held at once by the same people, because the tally is kept per class: Newton is absolutely true of the cart and absolutely wrong of the muon.[^13] "Newton is wrong" is a sentence about universality, about whether the theory is reliable on every class, and one reproducible class on which every trial counts against settles it in the negative while leaving every other class untouched.

Advice and personal truths run on the same tally and the same updates, and the truth they reach is of the same kind. They differ in three ways: their situations cannot be re-instanced at will, so each class collects few trials and belief stays near its prior; the trials are private, so nobody else's enter the urn; and they carry no hypothesis set, so nothing turns a failure into a decided trial against, and a failed piece of advice is blamed or excused as its holder pleases. The first two are differences of access, which a public, reproducible class removes by pooling everyone's trials into one urn. The third is a difference of structure, and it is why the problem of Section 6 is asked of theories and not of advice.

## 6. So what is the set?

If all of this is obvious once said, and I think most of it is, then the question it leaves is plain. What is the hypothesis set for Newtonian mechanics? Since a set belongs to a derivation, the question asked properly is for a generator: something that takes a theory and a derivation written out in full and returns the members, with the form of each member's test, before the observation is made, and not a list of excuses assembled after the fact. Where does "check this patch of sky" come from? I can say what the answer must involve, and no more. It is not in any equation. The member that counted for Newton in 1846 comes from the universality of the law, every body attracting every body, which makes any unaccounted residual the signature of an unaccounted body; from the rule, prior to any equation, that every force has a source; and from the record of residuals, which is not part of the theory at all and yet supplied the member's entire content. Nobody has written down the minimal structure from which such search demands follow, though the last three sections show that it exists, since it was obeyed. Three conditions would tell us when it had been written down: for a Newtonian derivation of an orbit it must yield the force-completeness member with its demand for a search, and say what the search is for; for the derivation of a Bell inequality from local realism it must yield the five loopholes below; and it must be invariant under regrouping, two derivations of the same prediction that differ only in how their commitments are cut up getting the same independent tests. That is a well-posed problem, and I pose it here rather than attempt it.[^14]

The question is not special to mechanics. Ask it of thermodynamics: when a derivation from the second law fails, which of "the system was not isolated", "it had not reached equilibrium", "the boundary was not adiabatic" and "the work was not all counted" does the theory's structure license, as opposed to a defender's ingenuity, and is the list the same for every derivation? I do not know, and I have not found the question asked.

Ask it of quantum mechanics, and something unusual appears: one community has written its set down, though not in advance. A Bell test derives, from local realism together with commitments about the apparatus and the analysis, an inequality that quantum mechanics violates. The commitments were named one by one, as loopholes, over forty years: detection, that the pairs detected are a fair sample of all the pairs (Pearle 1970, before the first test); locality, that the setting on one side could not have influenced the outcome on the other; memory, that earlier trials do not inform later ones (Barrett et al. 2002); coincidence-time, concerning how pairs are identified (Larsson and Gill 2004); and freedom of choice, that the settings were independent of whatever hidden variables there are, under that name only in 2010 (Scheidl et al. 2010; Hall 2010). Kofler, Giustina, Larsson and Mitchell (2016) list exactly these five as the loopholes to be closed simultaneously. Each was a commitment already silently in use, a coincidence window, a fair-sampling step, an assumption that trials are independent, which is how Section 3 said sets go unnamed; Giustina's data of 2013 were re-analysed the next year to close a coincidence-time loophole left open (Larsson et al. 2014). Read in my terms, the list is a hypothesis set for local realism, publicly maintained, and the forty-three years from the first test by Freedman and Clauser (1972) to 2015 are a window in which the members were named and rejected one by one: locality first addressed by Aspect, Dalibard and Roger (1982), detection closed with photons in 2013 (Giustina et al. 2013; Christensen et al. 2013), each main loophole by then closed but, as Giustina's abstract put it, "in different experiments".[^15] In 2015 three experiments closed detection and locality together (Hensen et al. 2015; Giustina et al. 2015; Shalm et al. 2015), with coincidence-time and memory handled by the definition of the trials and the statistics, and freedom of choice closed under an assumption about where the hidden variable originates. That the list was reached after the fact is the reason for asking whether it could have been read off in advance: the target exists, and it was found the slow way.

One member was never rejected and never will be, and it is a reading of a member just named. Measurement independence is one commitment with two strengths. On the first, the setting could have been correlated with the hidden variable through an ordinary causal channel; the field closes that by separating the choice from the emission at space-like distance and, in the cosmic tests, by pushing any common cause into the deep past (Handsteiner et al. 2017; Rauch et al. 2018). On the second, superdeterminism, settings and hidden variables share a common cause regardless of separation, and no Bell test can bear on it, since every test presupposes its falsity; Larsson's review (2014) singles it out as the one loophole that cannot be closed by scientific methods, and Hossenfelder and Palmer (2020) document both the community's agreement that it is unclosable and its refusal to take it seriously.[^16] In the terms of this paper, a member can be undecidable, and when it is, the community declares it outside the set by convention and applies the verdict rule to the rest; here the convention is about how much of one member the field agrees to test. The Bell list shows the set existing in practice wherever a field has had to say exactly what would have to be true for a theory to be wrong. But that list is the set for one derivation, of one inequality, from one theory's rival. What is quantum mechanics' own hypothesis set, for an ordinary prediction, a spectral line or a tunnelling rate? Nobody has written it down.

So the problem, stated plainly. A theory's hypothesis set is a definite object. It is fixed by the theory's structure together with the derivation used, and by convention where a member is undecidable, and it is in place before any observation is made. Its members decide, one by one, whether a trial has said anything about the theory, and until the last of them is decided the trial has said nothing. For no major theory has the set been written down. Writing it down is what it would take to say, in advance, what a failed prediction would have to be a failure of.
