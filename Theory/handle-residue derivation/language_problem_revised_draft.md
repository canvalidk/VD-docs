# The Language Problem and the Move of Perfection

*On first axioms, subjective flawlessness, and how inquiry begins*

## 1. The Language Problem

Some statements are treated as if they were made of gold.

"1 + 1 = 2."

"Gravity is real."

"The universe obeys laws."

Mathematics, science, and whole worldviews rest on statements of this kind. They are taught, defended, extended, placed under enormous conceptual weight.

Yet there is something humiliating about them. To a speaker outside the relevant language, "1 + 1 = 2" may be no more transparent than "pip plup pip plap pop." The nonsense string is not arbitrary: it preserves the visible repetition-pattern of the mathematical statement while lacking its interpretation. A foundational statement does not wear its foundation on its face.

Call this pressure the **language problem**.

The name is not an announcement of a new problem. It is a label for an old foundational difficulty: inquiry must begin with statements whose meaning, authority, and admissibility cannot themselves be fully secured in advance. Before interpretation begins, a theorem, an axiom, a law, and a piece of gibberish can look disturbingly similar. Each is, at the surface level, a pattern of marks, sounds, pixels, scratches, or vibrations.

The question is not merely why one string is true and another false. The prior question is why one string becomes eligible for truth or falsity at all.

Why does "1 + 1 = 2" enter the space of reasons, while the structurally similar "pip plup pip plap pop" is discarded as noise?

### The pre-Cartesian question

Descartes's project famously searches for a first certainty. By doubting everything that can be doubted, he hopes to find a proposition that cannot be shaken: *I think, therefore I am*. This is usually treated as a foundation for knowledge. But even this statement requires language to have already begun functioning. The words "I," "think," "therefore," and "am" must already be admissible. They must already be more than noise.

This is not a refutation of Descartes. It is a shift in level.

Descartes asks: *which statement can survive doubt?* The language problem asks: *how does a statement become the kind of thing that can be doubted, affirmed, denied, proved, or installed as an axiom?*

In that sense, the language problem is a pre-Cartesian version of the problem of first principles. Descartes searches for the first indubitable statement. The language problem asks how a statement earns entry into the arena where doubt and certainty are possible at all.

The same issue appears in axiomatics. A theory does not begin with arbitrary noise. It begins with statements such as "through any two points there is exactly one line," or "equal things added to equal things are equal," or "the speed of light in vacuum is constant." Before asking whether such claims are true, self-evident, useful, elegant, empirical, or conventional, there is a more basic question: what licenses them as candidate beginnings?

This is the problem of **axiom-admission**. A candidate axiom must somehow be distinguished from a meaningless inscription before its truth, necessity, or usefulness can be assessed.

### The impossibility of total clarification

One tempting answer is that a statement becomes legitimate once all its terms have been clarified. But this demand quickly becomes destructive.

Every statement depends on other statements. Every definition uses further terms. Every term can be challenged. If someone says "force equals mass times acceleration," another person can ask what is meant by force, then by mass, then by equality, then by meaning. Such questions are not automatically dishonest. Sometimes a side-term really is where the argument breaks.

But if every dependency must be fully settled before inquiry can proceed, inquiry cannot begin. Language can always interrupt itself.

What follows is an account of one move that sometimes lets inquiry begin anyway. The move does not solve the language problem. It addresses a specific part of it, under specific conditions, and those conditions can fail. It is offered here as a candidate, with a sketch of why it works, rather than as a completed solution.

## 2. The Move

Suppose two parties are trying to begin a discussion and find that they do not share a language on some term the discussion depends on. One party cannot, by their own authority, settle what the term should mean for the other; and the other cannot do the reverse.

There is a move available in this situation. One party can recognize that authority over how the term is used by the other lies with the other, and act on that recognition: they let the other's usage be the standard for what the term does, without attempting to impose their own.

Consider Newton trying to discuss how an apple responds to gravity. Galileo interrupts to ask what colour the apple is. This is not a bad-faith obstruction; it is a question Galileo takes to matter, and which Newton cannot answer on his own terms, because Newton has no independent theory of colour that Galileo would accept.

Newton's move is to let the apple have whatever colour Galileo's standards would require. If red satisfies Galileo, the apple is red. If Galileo's account requires something more elaborate, the apple satisfies that account. Newton is not claiming to know the colour. He is letting Galileo's authority over the colour-question fix the matter, so the discussion of gravity can continue.

The move has a specific structure. Newton is not deferring the question (saying "let's come back to colour later"). He is not stipulating a value (saying "assume the apple is red"). He is not bracketing the question (saying "the gravity analysis is colour-independent"). He is doing something stronger: he is letting Galileo's completed treatment of the colour-question fix what the apple's colour is for the purposes of this discussion.

The proposal of this essay is to couple this move to the word **perfection**. "The apple is the perfect colour" is, under the proposal, an utterance whose content is that the apple's colour is whatever Galileo's standards would finally require. Perfection names the move.

This is a proposal, not a discovery. Ordinary English uses "perfect" in loosely adjacent ways — "that's perfect" as acknowledgment that something meets the hearer's requirements is close in flavour — but the technical sense proposed here is a stipulation. The word is chosen because it resonates with existing usages and because, as the next section argues, the move genuinely produces flawlessness in a specific sense. But the reader should not assume the coupling is already present in their understanding of the word. It is being introduced.

## 3. Subjective Flawlessness

The word "perfect" earns its place through a specific logical property of the move.

Once Newton has let Galileo's standards fix the apple's colour, Galileo cannot coherently object to the apple's colour. Any objection he would raise would have to be an objection against his own standards, because the colour of the apple is, by construction, whatever those standards require. An apple-colour Galileo would object to is not the colour the apple has; the colour the apple has is the one Galileo's completed treatment would endorse.

This is not a rhetorical flourish or a polite evasion. It is a logical consequence of the move. The objection-space on the colour-question has been closed for Galileo — not by answering his objections, but by yielding him authority over what the answer is.

The apple is, in this specific sense, flawless with respect to Galileo and the colour-dependency. Call this **subjective flawlessness**. The term is not ideal, because "subjective" in philosophy can suggest "merely subjective," meaning arbitrary or non-authoritative. That is not the meaning here. The subjectivity in subjective flawlessness is not a weakness of the flawlessness; it is the specification of its scope. The flawlessness is genuine and logically closed. It simply holds with respect to a particular subject — the subject whose standards were yielded to — rather than absolutely.

This is what distinguishes perfection from adjacent moves. Deferral postpones criticism but does not foreclose it: Galileo can still return to the colour-question later and find the apple wanting. Stipulation assigns a value but leaves the stipulation itself open to attack: if Newton simply declares the apple red, Galileo can still object that red is wrong. Bracketing sets a dependency aside but can be unbracketed: "the analysis is colour-independent" invites Galileo to show that it is not. Perfection forecloses criticism on the yielded dependency, from the party to whom authority was yielded, for the duration of the yielding.

That is a distinctive logical property, and it is what the word "perfect" picks out in this technical sense. The flawlessness is real, it is relative, and it is earned by the structure of the move.

Two features of the flawlessness deserve note. It is indexed: the same object can be perfect-for-Galileo and not perfect for someone else whose standards were not yielded to. There is no contradiction here, because the flawlessness is relational. And it is temporary: it holds for the duration of the yielding, and if Newton later reclaims authority over the colour-question, the flawlessness lapses. Perfection is a state of the relation between the object and the subject, sustained by the move that produced it.

## 4. Failure Modes

The move can fail, and the ways it can fail are evidence that it has internal structure rather than being a rhetorical gesture.

**Divergence.** The objector's standard does not converge on anything. Each time it seems to settle, it moves. If someone says "let *S* be the largest number," and the objector replies "what about one million and one," and the speaker yields, and the objector replies "what about that plus one," the process does not terminate. There is no standard to yield *to*, because the objector's standard keeps producing new demands. Perfection cannot complete, and the attempt returns failure.

**Type-collapse.** The objector's completed standard, when it arrives, reveals that the original object cannot exist as described. "The perfect largest number" need not be a number. The recipient of authority might say it does not exist; or that the correct object is something like $\aleph_0$; or that the phrase was malformed. Perfection is not object-preserving: its output may be an object, a retyping, a nonexistence claim, a replacement, or a diagnosis of incoherence. This is not a bug. It is what happens when yielded authority is actually exercised.

**Oscillation.** The objector's standard switches between incompatible demands. The speaker yields, the objector supplies one criterion; the speaker yields again later, and the objector supplies a different and incompatible one. No stable flawlessness results because no stable standard is being held.

**Path-dependence.** Different routes of clarification lead to incompatible resolutions. If the speaker yields authority over the colour first and the shape second, they get one apple; if they yield shape first and colour second, they get a different apple. The order of yielding should not matter if the standard is coherent, so path-dependence signals that it is not.

**Goal-divergence.** The objector is not trying to reach a standard. They raise objections in order to prevent the discussion, not to clarify it. Suppose a different interlocutor than Galileo — a sophist, call him — interrupts Newton about the apple's colour, then its texture, then its smell, then the history of the tree. Each concession produces a new objection. The problem here is not that the standard is unstable or that the type is wrong; the problem is that there is no standard being aimed at, and therefore no target for yielding. The move requires the objector to *have*, or to be trying to develop, a standard that can close. Without that, nothing is available to yield to, and the attempted perfection dissolves.

Each failure mode calls for a different response. Divergence and oscillation may resolve if the objector's standard is given time to stabilize. Type-collapse is informative: it tells the speaker that what they were trying to discuss does not survive serious treatment and needs to be reformulated. Path-dependence suggests the standards at play are not jointly coherent and will need work before yielding is possible. Goal-divergence means the move should not be attempted with this interlocutor. Collapsing these into "the move failed" loses information the concept is built to preserve.

## 5. Perfection as a First Move

The move described here is sometimes presented as a rescue — something deployed when a within-language discussion gets stuck. That framing is misleading. The move is most fundamentally a **first** move: something available, and often necessary, when the parties do not yet share a language at all.

If you do not share a language with the other party, you cannot critique their usage — not as a matter of politeness, but as a matter of standing. You lack the authority. Any critique you would mount would have to be phrased in their usage, which you do not yet command, or in yours, which does not bind them. The move formalizes this situation. You are not failing to critique; you are acknowledging, by construction, that the authority over their usage lies with them, and letting their standards fix the relevant matters accordingly.

Once done, you can no longer critique them on those matters, and that is the point. The shared language that gets built is built on foundations that are protected from your attack in specific places. That protection is what lets the foundation support weight. Without it, every attempt at shared usage could be reopened indefinitely, and nothing could be built.

This requires acknowledging a scope condition honestly. The move is not absolutely first. It requires enough pre-existing shared structure for the word "perfect" itself — or its functional equivalent — to serve as the handoff signal. There must be enough common ground for the gesture of yielding to be recognized. The move is something like the first substantive linguistic move, riding on whatever pre-linguistic or minimally-linguistic recognition lets two parties notice they are trying to talk.

Earlier framings that describe perfection as "yielding authority" can be misleading on this point. The authority over the other party's usage was never the speaker's to begin with. What the move does is act on the recognition of where the authority lies. "Yielding" is a reasonable word for this if understood carefully, but the deeper structure is acknowledgment, not transfer. The authority was always theirs; the move is the first act that treats it as such.

## 6. A Provisional Proof Sketch

The claim this essay has been building toward is that the move described in Sections 2–5 addresses part of the language problem described in Section 1. What follows is a sketch of why, offered as provisional rather than as a completed argument.

The language problem, at the level relevant here, asks how parties who do not share a language can begin inquiry together. The difficulty is that neither party has authority over the other's usage, and appeals to shared standards presuppose the very sharing that is in question.

The move addresses this by letting one party's standards fix contested matters without the other party needing to first agree on what those matters mean. The logical closure property ensures that the result is stable in a specific sense: the yielded dependency can no longer be used to block inquiry from the objecting side, because objections on that dependency are pre-accommodated. Inquiry can then proceed on the remaining dependencies, and further applications of the move can address those in turn.

This is not a complete solution. It does not explain how the move itself becomes available — how the word "perfect," or its equivalent, is recognized by both parties as performing the handoff. It does not explain what "the other party's standards" means formally, or how yielding composes when multiple dependencies are in play. It does not address cases where the asymmetry of authority is itself contested, nor cases where the standards on both sides are mutually incoherent.

A full argument would require a precise account of several things: what authority over a usage consists in; how it can be yielded from inside a language system; what counts as a standard closing, converging, or remaining open; how the partial closures produced by successive applications of the move compose into shared usage robust enough to support inquiry. These are available in directions the author has developed elsewhere, but are not developed here.

What this essay delivers is narrower: a candidate move, a specification of the property that earns its name, an analysis of its failure modes, and a sketch of why it addresses part of the problem. This is offered as sufficient to motivate the move as a serious candidate, not as a completed proof.

## 7. Closing

The language problem is the difficulty that foundational statements do not wear their foundation on their face. To someone outside the relevant language, a theorem and a nonsense string are surface-indistinguishable, and the question of how one earns entry to the space of reasons while the other is discarded as noise is prior to any question of which is true.

The move of perfection is one thing speakers sometimes do inside this difficulty. It does not solve the language problem. It lets inquiry begin, in specific circumstances, by one party letting another's standards fix a contested matter so completely that objection on that matter is foreclosed. The flawlessness this produces is subjective — indexed to the subject whose authority was yielded to — but it is real, and it is earned by the structure of the move.

What becomes possible once the move is available is a further question. The admission of first terms into a theory, the stabilization of primitives, the construction of shared technical languages across communities that started without them — these are applications that the move makes tractable, but delivering them requires machinery beyond what has been developed here.

The modest claim is this: foundational statements do not wear their profundity on their face, and inquiry has to begin somewhere. One of the ways it begins is by one party yielding authority over a dependency to another in a way that makes objection on that dependency no longer available. That is a small thing, and local, and fails in identifiable ways. But it is concrete enough to name, and perhaps concrete enough to build on.
